When Supermarket Freezers Become Cyberattack Targets: The Hidden Risks of Internet-Connected Refrigeration

In an increasingly connected world, cyberattacks are no longer limited to computers, smartphones, and banking systems. Even supermarket refrigerators and freezers have become potential targets for cybercriminals.

On November 2, 2026, refrigeration-system incidents affected supermarkets across several European countries, including Belgium, Luxembourg, Sweden, Ireland, Estonia, Finland, and the Netherlands.

In Belgium, approximately 40 Delhaize stores and 15 Carrefour locations reported disruptions. In Ireland, 13 SuperValu stores were affected. Similar incidents were reported by other retailers across Europe.

Some refrigeration systems experienced unusual temperature increases, forcing retailers to remove or discard food products to protect consumers. Several incidents were attributed to cyberattacks, although investigations into the events and their causes were ongoing.

According to Reuters' report on the incident , the disruptions affected supermarket refrigeration systems across multiple European countries.

But this incident raises an interesting question: Why are supermarket refrigerators connected to the internet in the first place?

Why Are Supermarket Refrigerators Connected to the Internet?

Modern supermarkets rely on Internet of Things (IoT) technology to manage their refrigeration systems. These smart systems continuously monitor temperatures, control cooling operations, detect equipment failures, and notify staff when something goes wrong.

Internet connectivity allows employees to monitor refrigerators remotely instead of manually checking every freezer. It also helps supermarkets maintain food safety, reduce energy consumption, and improve operational efficiency.

However, the same connectivity that makes these systems convenient can also create security vulnerabilities.

How Can Hackers Target Refrigeration Systems?

Like other internet-connected devices, smart refrigeration systems use software, sensors, networks, and digital controllers. If these systems have weak passwords, outdated software, exposed remote-access services, or insufficient security protections, attackers may find opportunities to gain unauthorized access.

Once inside, attackers could potentially interfere with temperature monitoring, cooling controls, or system operations. This could result in food spoilage, operational disruptions, financial losses, and potential food safety concerns.

The November incidents demonstrate how attacks against connected operational technology can have consequences in the physical world, not just in digital information systems.

The Bigger Problem: Security Risks in IoT

The Internet of Things is transforming industries by connecting everyday devices to digital networks. From smart homes and healthcare equipment to industrial machines and supermarket refrigeration, connected technology is becoming increasingly common.

However, security is not always given the same priority as convenience. Some IoT devices operate with limited security features, outdated firmware, or inadequate access controls.

A vulnerability in one connected device can potentially create risks for an entire organization's network, particularly when devices are not properly isolated.

How Can These Systems Be Protected?

Organizations should take several precautions to reduce cybersecurity risks:

  • Use strong, unique passwords and multi-factor authentication.
  • Regularly update device firmware and software.
  • Separate refrigeration and operational technology systems from business and payment networks.
  • Monitor network activity for unusual behaviour.
  • Restrict remote access to authorized personnel.
  • Conduct regular security assessments and vulnerability testing.
  • Maintain manual monitoring and incident-response procedures in case connected systems become unavailable.

Conclusion

The cyber incidents affecting European supermarkets are a reminder that cybersecurity extends far beyond traditional computer systems. Devices designed to preserve food can also become targets when they are connected to the internet without adequate protection.

As IoT technology continues to expand, organizations must recognize that every connected device can introduce potential security risks. Convenience and automation are valuable, but they should never come at the cost of security.

The future of technology is connected, but that future must also be secure.
